An equated monthly installment is a regular payment designed to cover interest and reduce a loan balance over a chosen term. The payment can remain constant while its internal split changes each month. Understanding that split helps explain why early payments often contain more interest and why a lower monthly payment can still lead to a larger total cost.

The [EMI calculator](/calculators/emi/) models a fixed nominal annual rate, equal monthly periods and a fixed repayment term. Its defaults are examples, not offers. Fees, insurance, taxes, irregular dates and rate changes are outside the basic model. Use the output to understand a scenario, then compare it with the actual agreement and schedule.

## Translate the inputs into monthly terms

Let P be the starting principal, r the monthly rate written as a decimal and n the number of payments. If a hypothetical nominal annual rate is 12%, the model uses r = 0.12 / 12 = 0.01. A twelve-month term gives n = 12. Do not enter twelve years as twelve months; unit mistakes can dwarf any rounding difference.

For a positive rate, the payment is P × r / (1 − (1 + r) raised to −n). This is an annuity formula. At a zero rate, the expression needs a separate treatment: payment is simply P / n. The calculator handles that edge case rather than dividing by a quantity that becomes zero.

## Work through one illustrative payment

Suppose the principal is INR 1,00,000, the example annual rate is 12% and the term is twelve months. The modeled EMI is approximately INR 8,884.88. In the first month, interest is 1% of INR 1,00,000, or INR 1,000. The remainder of the payment, about INR 7,884.88, reduces principal.

The next month begins with approximately INR 92,115.12. Interest is now calculated on that smaller balance, so the interest portion decreases. With the total payment held constant, the principal portion increases. The schedule repeats this process until the balance is paid. Display rounding can make a hand calculation differ by a few paise from unrounded internal arithmetic.

## Read the amortization table as a sequence

The opening balance, interest, principal repaid and closing balance should tell a consistent story. Opening balance plus interest minus payment gives the next balance in this simplified model. The principal portion across the schedule should add up to the original principal, subject to display rounding. The interest column explains the cost generated by the assumed rate and timing.

Use the table’s page controls to inspect both early and late payments. Looking only at the first row can create an exaggerated impression of the interest share over the entire loan. Looking only at the final row can hide how slowly the balance fell at the start. The full sequence is the useful object.

## Compare a longer term carefully

Keep principal and rate fixed, then increase the number of months. The EMI generally falls because repayment is spread over more periods. However, the balance remains outstanding for longer, so total modeled interest can rise. Compare both the monthly obligation and the sum of payments when exploring affordability.

The [percentage calculator](/calculators/percentage/) can help express the difference between two payments or totals, but define the base clearly. A reduction measured against the old payment is different from the new payment as a percentage of the old one. Always label what the percentage describes.

## Explore prepayment as a separate scenario

The [loan prepayment calculator](/calculators/loan-prepayment/) models extra payments while keeping the EMI fixed. Reducing principal sooner can reduce future interest and shorten the modeled term. This is a scenario comparison, not a statement that every loan allows the same prepayment behaviour or charges nothing for it.

Check how a real lender applies additional payments. Some arrangements change the installment, some change the term, and some involve conditions or charges. The calculator’s assumption is visible so that you can avoid comparing incompatible schedules. Do not subtract modeled savings from a real budget without accounting for costs the model excludes.

## Use the result as an explanation, not a quote

Currency selection changes the display label; it does not convert exchange rates. Indian grouping helps read lakh and crore amounts, but the arithmetic is the same under international grouping. Very large numbers should be checked for entry mistakes before interpreting a dramatic result.

Keep a record of the principal, rate, months and assumptions used when comparing scenarios. A screenshot of a result without its inputs is difficult to interpret later. For a real borrowing decision, obtain the lender’s repayment schedule and full cost information, and resolve differences in conventions before relying on either set of figures.
